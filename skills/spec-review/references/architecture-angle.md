# Architecture angle

Read the spec without changing it, and look for what is missing for the architecture to hold.

- **Implicit qualities**: volume, response time, availability, security and personal data, traceability, accessibility, the platforms to support, operability (logs, backups, restoring). Propose each one to the product owner as a measurable non-functional requirement, with the template of `non-functional-requirements.md`: the situation (which machine, which data, which load), what is expected and its threshold, with a proportion ("the week shows in under a second 9 times out of 10, with five years of data"), and what a miss costs the business. A vague word ("fast", "scalable") or a "never" becomes a question with a default value the user only has to confirm or correct. A composite quality ("evolvable") breaks down into measurable ones (time to deliver a change, deployability, testability). The tester verifies the requirement; an architecture rule that serves it is checked by a fitness function, which you write (skill `architecture-tests`). The product owner files the requirement; the architect keeps the ranking of the qualities that drive their decisions, and the ADRs that say how the architecture holds them.
- **External systems**: does a new system appear? What does it guarantee (availability, format, delays)? Who owns it, and what is the relationship with that owner? If they take requests from us, the slice can ask for what it needs; if they do not, we either take their model as it is or translate it at the boundary (an anticorruption layer). This decides how much translation the slice carries, and whether the slice depends on someone else's schedule: say it.
- **What the existing platform already provides**: before asking for or recording a cross-cutting mechanism (timeout, retry, authentication, translating errors to HTTP), check who already provides it: the infrastructure (API gateway, service mesh) and the in-house libraries on the classpath (global exception handler, default HTTP client, default timeouts). Doubling it creates two settings that drift apart; ignoring it creates conflicts (two handlers for the same exception). Write it in the instructions.
- **Bounded contexts**: does the slice stay within one context, or does it cross a boundary? Does the spec's vocabulary match a single model? A word that means something else elsewhere in the product (a "customer" for sales and for support) signals a boundary.
- **What must be true at once, and what can be true a little later**: for a rule that ties two things together ("the stock never goes below zero", "the loyalty points are credited for every order"), ask whether it must hold at the very moment of the action or whether a short delay is acceptable, and which delay. It decides what changes together in one transaction. A rule that seems to require changing two things at once is often satisfied by a delay the business accepts: ask before designing for the instant.
- **Technical uncertainty**: if a technical question can invalidate the slice ("does the supplier's API filter by date?", "do we handle 10,000 rows in under 2 seconds?"), propose a **timeboxed investigation**: a short task, its duration fixed in advance, whose output is an answer and not code to keep (the product owner calls it a *spike*).
- **What is delivered, and to whom**: before working out a data migration, backup compatibility or an upgrade scenario, ask which versions are in users' hands. Numbered migrations exist to bring a database in service up to date without losing its data: as long as no database is in service outside the development machines, no compatibility is owed, and the schema can be rewritten in place until the first delivery, provided you say how to reset the development machines. Once delivered, a migration is never modified. Before resting a decision on resetting the development machine's data, check where development stores it: if it shares the storage of the installed application or of another environment (same data folder, same local database, same account), propose to separate them by environment, chosen by the application itself rather than by a launch script, so that development never damages any data but its own. This check holds outside the review too, whenever a decision rests on resetting data.
- **Splitting**: can the slice cross every layer without building everything? An in-memory adapter in place of an external system often lets a first slice ship sooner.
- **Walking skeleton**: when the spec is the first slice of a new or inherited product, check that it includes the automated chain (build, packaging, deployment to a realistic environment, one end-to-end test on the packaged application), not only the feature: that chain is what brings the questions of structure and environment out early. An uncertain link (a native module, a test tool, code signing) becomes a timeboxed investigation. A sketch of the skeleton's structure, a few minutes at the whiteboard, is enough: it is a starting point, not a detailed design.

The remarks go to the product owner, worded as needs or questions, never as a solution to write into the spec.
